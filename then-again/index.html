The pattern

Keep the look. Change what is inside.

Every comeback on this site involved the same two decisions. What happens to the look, and what happens to the part that did the work.

Jameson calls this move pastiche, an old style copied with no joke in it (64). The table shows how regular it is. The surface survives because people recognise it. The working part goes because a newer one is cheaper or easier to sell.

The argument

How retro design turns the past into something to sell

People in the past kept drawing the future. People now keep buying back the past. This page explains how the two are connected.

A future that became a picture

Mid-century magazines promised flying cars and helpful robots. We are still in traffic. When we look at those drawings today, they no longer tell us what is coming. They show us how an earlier generation pictured tomorrow. Jameson says the past now reaches us as a collection of images (66). A design that once pointed forward becomes one of those images, and people call the result retro-futuristic.

The cycle

Older generations looked forward. Many people now look back, often through new technology. Berman describes modern life as “a maelstrom of perpetual disintegration and renewal” (15). The printed date on a film photo is a small example. It was a new feature once. Phone cameras replaced it, and then apps brought it back as an effect. The object went away, but its look returned inside the thing that replaced it. Berman also suggests that going back can be a way to go forward (36).

Why companies want it

Products turn over fast, and a company has to keep offering something that feels fresh. Jameson argues that companies need a steady supply of goods that seem new, which gives design an economic job (56). A design does not have to be new to do that job. It only has to be uncommon in daily life. A shape from decades ago can still be presented as a new release. Jameson’s word for this kind of borrowing is pastiche, a copy of an older style with no joke in it (64).

Who it is for

Retro products can look alike, yet small differences make them appeal to different buyers. Adorno and Horkheimer argue that such differences help companies sort people into groups, even when the products follow one formula (97). One buyer wants the real film camera. Another only wants the film look on a phone. The authors also doubt that companies simply give people what they already want (95). Seeing familiar products again and again shapes what people expect. Companies then point to that expectation as the reason to make more.

What this site shows

Each object here has two sides. Turn one over and you move from its own time to the year it was sold again. In the cases we studied, memories of the past return as products, and companies earn new value from them. The culture industry does not only sell nostalgia. It helps create the wish for it.

How it was built

One page of HTML, CSS and JavaScript with no framework. The darkroom changes your photo pixel by pixel on a canvas, in your own browser. Its two steps, film first and then the print, follow the idea behind spektrafilm by Andrea Volpato and the SpektraLab app. No code or film data from either project is used, and the looks are approximations, not measured film stocks. The globe and the marbled ground are drawn on canvas. Sound uses the Web Audio API, and the halos follow the volume of the recording. The site works with a keyboard and respects the reduced motion setting.
